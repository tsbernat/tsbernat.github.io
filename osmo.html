Osmoregulatory Plasticity in a Comparative Killifish System

In a world increasingly characterized by major disturbances including extreme weather events and global pandemics, it is critical to address questions about how populations remain resilient. Phenotypic plasticity is the capacity for an individual to exhibit high flexibility in their behavior and physiology depending on the environmental context and is a key mechanism for responding to variable and disturbed environments. Despite its importance, mechanisms that support physiological flexibility remain poorly understood. Euryhalinity is the physiological ability to regulate internal salt and water balance across a wide range of salinities; this is an important form of reversible phenotypic plasticity that equips organisms to acclimatize to environments with periodic and stochastic fluctuations in salinity. Sea level rise, salt intrusions, and more intense rainfall events from global climate change all influence coastal ecosystems’ salt levels, one of the most fundamentally limiting environmental features of almost all aquatic species’ ranges.

The Fundulus genus of killifish includes diverse osmoregulatory (body fluid regulation) physiologies distributed among closely related species, where euryhaline lineages have repeatedly and independently radiated from marine into freshwater environments, accompanied by a loss of euryhalinity (defined as stenohalinity). These replicated losses of phenotypic flexibility provide a unique opportunity to dissect whether this physiological variation is governed by random neutral drift or adaptive constraint, achieving a high scope of inference about the genomic architecture of euryhalinity in fish. I aim to integrate genetic, molecular, and physiological information to discover mechanisms that enable euryhalinity using the Fundulus killifish model system.
